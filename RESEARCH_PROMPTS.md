# Deep-research prompts

Prompts to run in Gemini Deep Research (or any long-form research agent). Each one is self-contained.
Run one per morning, read it in the evening, and fold the findings into the site (About, Rights,
country pages) and into [STRATEGY.md](STRATEGY.md) section 10. Save each result as a `.docx` in the
git-ignored `internunion_Google deep research prompts/` folder and summarise provenance in
`data/SOURCES.md`.

Shared instructions to paste at the top of every prompt:

> You are a senior policy researcher. Cite every factual claim with a source and a date. Distinguish
> peer-reviewed evidence, official statistics, official documents, NGO reports and press. Flag anything
> you could not verify. Prefer 2019–2026 sources; older only for history. Give figures in EUR with the
> year. End with a table of the ten strongest sources and a list of open questions. Do not pad.

---

## 1. The economic case: why paying interns matters for workforce formation

Research the evidence, 2010–2026, EU, OECD and US, on how internship pay affects (a) who takes
internships (social background, region, gender), (b) later employment, earnings and "scarring"
effects of unpaid versus paid placements, (c) retention and conversion of interns into hires,
(d) productivity and quality of work by trainees, and (e) the macro cost of unpaid internships to
families and to public budgets. Include Eurobarometer and Eurofound findings on traineeships, the
Commission's impact assessment for the 2024 Traineeships Directive proposal, and any national
evaluations of pay floors (France, Italy, Spain). Distinguish causal studies from correlations.
Output: an evidence table by claim, with strength of evidence, and five one-sentence talking points
that a centre-right and a centre-left politician could each say without embarrassment.

## 2. Integrity and security: financial precarity of trainees in sensitive institutions

Research how personnel-security and integrity frameworks (EU institutions' security rules, NATO and
national vetting guidance, corporate insider-risk literature) treat financial stress and debt as a
risk indicator; documented cases where interns or junior staff in public institutions, consultancies,
or law firms were approached by foreign or commercial interests; and how institutions that pay
trainees justify it in integrity terms. Cover Brussels specifically: what access Blue Book, Schuman,
Council, agency and political-group trainees have, and what the institutions' own rules say about
trainee vetting. Keep the tone factual and avoid alarmism. Output: a sourced brief of no more than
1,500 words plus three talking points suitable for a security-minded audience.

## 3. A national duty: demography, cohesion and the first rung of the career ladder

Research how governments and think tanks of the centre-right and conservative traditions across
Europe frame investment in young workers' first job: demographic decline, youth emigration from
southern and eastern member states, "brain drain" to Brussels and to northern capitals, and skills
shortages. Find which national programmes (apprenticeship guarantees, youth guarantee schemes,
first-job tax credits, hiring bonuses after internships such as Spain's and Italy's) have measured
results. Identify the vocabulary these actors use so the argument can be made in their words.
Output: a framing memo with quotes and sources, and a map of which member states already treat
unpaid internships as a competitiveness problem.

## 4. Comparative law: what equilibria exist, and how interns are kept afterwards

Build a comparative table for all EU-27 plus the UK, Switzerland, Norway, the US, Canada and
Australia: legal definition of an internship or traineeship; whether pay is mandatory and at what
floor (amount, formula, indexation); exemptions (curricular, duration thresholds); enforcement body
and sanctions; social-security coverage; and any instrument that encourages hiring the intern after
the placement (conversion bonuses, tax credits, quotas). Add the international-organisation layer:
EU institutions, UN system, Council of Europe, OECD, NATO. Note the date of each rule and pending
reforms, including the EU Traineeships Directive trilogue and the Belgian follow-up to the European
Committee of Social Rights decision in European Youth Forum v Belgium. Output: one table, one page of
patterns ("what the countries that pay well have in common"), and the three models that could be
proposed as a Belgian or EU standard.

## 5. Political mapping: who says what, and what would unite them

Map positions on intern pay and the EU Traineeships Directive by European political family (EPP,
S&D, Renew, Greens/EFA, ECR, Patriots, The Left, ESN) and by the main national parties in Belgium,
France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Poland and the Netherlands, using votes, amendments, manifestos and
statements 2023–2026. Map employer and social-partner positions: BusinessEurope, SMEunited, HOTREC,
Eurochambres, ETUC, CESI, Eurocadres, and the Belgian FEB/VBO, UCM, Unizo, FGTB, CSC. For each actor,
list the objection they raise and the argument that has moved similar actors elsewhere. Output: a
matrix of actors × arguments, a list of "bridge" positions at least three families share, and the
objections we must be able to answer in one sentence each.

## 6. History of intern movements: what worked and what died

Document, with dates and sources, the intern-rights campaigns since 2000: Génération Précaire (FR),
La Repubblica degli Stagisti and its "Bollino OK Stage" (IT), Intern Aware (UK), Plattform Generation
Praktikum (AT), the 2013 Brussels Sandwich Protest, InternsGoPro, the Brussels Interns NGO, the Fair
Internship Initiative, Pay Our Interns (US), Interns Australia, the Canadian Intern Association, the
European Youth Forum's collective complaint against Belgium, the European Ombudsman's EEAS decision
(2017), the European Parliament's 2019 decision on MEP-office trainees, and the Quality Framework for
Traineeships and the Traineeships Directive up to the current state of the trilogue. For each: goal,
tactic, data product if any, institutional partners, outcome, and why it faded or persisted. Confirm
the present-day status of each organisation and name any current contact channel. Output: a timeline,
a "lessons" section, and a list of organisations still active with what they do now.

## 7. The Brussels ecosystem: allies, events, calendar

Compile an organisation-level directory (no personal data beyond public office-holders) of Brussels
actors relevant to internship pay: youth organisations, union youth structures, trainee committees of
each EU institution and how they are elected, MEPs and offices working on the traineeships file,
Brussels-Region bodies administering the CIP, university student services and career services, civic-
tech and open-data groups, journalists and outlets that have covered intern pay since 2022. Add a
calendar for October 2026 to June 2027 of recurring moments: institutional trainee intakes, International
Interns' Day, European Youth Week, European Youth Event, relevant Parliament committee sessions, rent
barometer publications. Output: a directory table (name, what they do, why relevant, public channel),
and a dated calendar.

## 8. Legal risk for a public employer-response ledger in Belgium

Research, for a Belgian-hosted open-data project that publishes organisation-level statements such as
"questionnaire sent on DATE, no reply as of DATE" and "disclosed stipend below the livable floor":
Belgian defamation and calumny law and civil liability for reputational harm; the right of reply
(droit de réponse) and how it applies online; GDPR treatment of professional contact addresses and
of intern testimonies; database rights over the EU Transparency Register and over organisations' own
web pages; the legality and etiquette of low-frequency automated fetching of official pages; and the
precautions taken by comparable projects (AskTheEU, WhatDoTheyKnow, LobbyFacts, Repubblica degli
Stagisti, Glassdoor's EU operations). Output: a risk register with likelihood, impact and a concrete
mitigation for each, plus model wording for the four response states and for a corrections policy.

## 9. How civic-data projects grow: distribution tactics that worked

Study how comparable projects reached their audience without a marketing budget: LobbyFacts, AskTheEU,
Repubblica degli Stagisti, Pay Our Interns, Levels.fyi in its first year, the Fair Internship Initiative,
OpenSpending, and two or three national pay-transparency campaigns. For each: the launch moment, the
one artefact that travelled (a chart, a list, a badge), the first institutional partner, the press
strategy, and which social channels mattered. Add what is known in 2025–2026 about how LinkedIn,
Instagram, TikTok and Bluesky treat data-visual and document posts from small accounts. Output: a
playbook of ten tactics ranked by effort-to-reach, adapted to a maintainer with 30 minutes a day.

## 10. The Brussels cost-of-living floor, refreshed

Update the livable-floor inputs used on the site for 2026–2027: shared-room and studio rents in the
Brussels-Capital Region by commune (Federia/Brukot barometers, Statbel, university housing data),
utilities, groceries, STIB fares including the under-25 tariff, health insurance and phone, and the
CIP indexed minimum. Compare against the Blue Book, Schuman and Council stipends and against the
Eurostat at-risk-of-poverty threshold for a single adult in Belgium. Output: a one-table update with
sources and dates that can replace the constants in `build.py` and `data/SOURCES.md` directly.

## 11. Representation: can interns be unionised in Brussels and in the EU institutions?

Establish what collective representation of interns is legally possible in Brussels, and what has
actually been built anywhere. Four layers, each answered separately.

**(a) Belgian law.** For each status an intern in Brussels can hold — *convention d'immersion
professionnelle* / beroepsinlevingsovereenkomst (CIP/BIO), curricular internship under a school
agreement, Erasmus+ traineeship, IBO/FPI, volunteer under the 2005 volunteering law, student
employment contract, and ordinary employment contract — determine: whether the person may join a
trade union and be assisted by it; whether they are covered by a sectoral collective agreement
(check at least CP/PC 200 for white-collar staff and CP/PC 329 for the socio-cultural sector, which
between them cover most Brussels NGOs, associations, think tanks and trade associations); whether
they count toward the headcount thresholds for a *délégation syndicale*, a CPPT and a works council,
and whether they may vote or stand in the social elections; what protection exists against
retaliation or non-renewal for union activity; and what the minimum allowance is, how it is indexed,
and who enforces it. State the legal instrument and its date for every answer.

**(b) The EU institutions.** Determine the exact legal status of Blue Book, Schuman, Council, EEAS,
agency, political-group and MEP-office trainees relative to the Staff Regulations and the Conditions
of Employment of Other Servants. Then: may the recognised staff unions and professional associations
(Union Syndicale, R&D, Generation 2004, FFPE, Solidarité Européenne, TAO-AFI and others) admit or
represent trainees, and has any of them tried? What are the trainee committees of each institution —
how are they constituted, how are they elected, what are they formally entitled to do, and who do
they speak to? Is there any mechanism resembling collective consultation for trainees? Cover the
European Ombudsman's 2017 EEAS decision, the European Parliament's 2019 Bureau decision on MEP-office
trainees, and any complaint, petition or case brought by trainees since 2015 and its outcome.

**(c) Precedents anywhere.** Document every serious attempt to organise interns, trainees or
comparable non-employees, with dates, sources and outcomes: FNV Young & United (NL) and how it won;
ver.di Jugend and the German *Praktikum* campaigns; ÖGB/ÖGJ (AT); CGT and CFDT stagiaire structures
(FR); NIdiL-CGIL and the Italian atypical-work unions; ICTU Youth (IE); UK graduate and intern
organising after Intern Aware; US graduate-worker and congressional-intern organising; and, in
Belgium specifically, **CSC United Freelancers** and the FGTB's equivalent structures for
self-employed and platform workers — how they were founded, what legal form they took, how they are
financed, how they solved the problem that their members are not employees, and how many members
they hold. Say plainly which of these survived and which did not.

**(d) What could be built here, and what it would cost.** Compare the realistic vehicles: a trainee
section inside an existing Belgian union; an associate-membership category; a network hosted by a
union youth wing; an independent ASBL with a cooperation protocol with one or more unions; a purely
informational project with no representative claim. For each: legal requirements, what it may and
may not call itself under Belgian law on trade-union denomination and collective bargaining, money
and staff needed, time to set up, and the specific risk to an individual intern who fronts it.
Address the structural obstacle directly: Brussels internships run in five-month cohorts, and most
bubble employers sit below every Belgian threshold for workplace representation.

**Output.** A table for (a) by status × right; a two-page brief for (b) naming the bodies that exist
and what they can do; a precedent table for (c) with founding date, legal form, member count and
current status; and for (d) a one-page recommendation with a first, second and third option, the
argument against each, and the three questions that must be put to a Belgian union lawyer before
anything is announced. Name, for each of (a) to (d), the organisation and public channel to verify
the answer with — no personal data beyond public office-holders.
